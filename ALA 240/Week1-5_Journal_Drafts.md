# Week 1 Journal: Intro to Well-being

## Part One

Before this class, my experience with journaling was limited and inconsistent. I sometimes wrote short notes about my study plans, emotions, or something difficult that happened during the day, but I usually treated writing as a way to record information rather than a way to understand myself. I also tended to stop when my schedule became busy. Because English is not my first language, I sometimes worried that a personal journal had to sound polished. That worry made writing feel like another assignment instead of a private space.

The journaling slideshow changed that understanding. My biggest takeaway is that a journal can be a judgment-free zone for reflection, planning, awareness, and autonomy. It does not have to follow one correct format. Free writing, prompts, bullet points, gratitude, and even hobby-based entries can all be useful if they help me notice what I think and feel. I also liked the suggestion to write for a limited amount of time and to reread older entries. A short, consistent practice may reveal patterns that are difficult to see in one busy day. Journaling can help me recognize what gives me energy, what drains me, and what I may be avoiding.

To engage fully in this class, I need to set aside perfectionism, comparison, and the belief that reflection must immediately produce a solution. I often compare my English, grades, and progress with classmates, especially in computer science. That comparison can make me hide uncertainty. I also need to suspend the assumption that difficult emotions are signs of weakness. If I write only what sounds positive, I will miss the information that discomfort is trying to give me. My journal should be honest enough to show confusion while still being respectful and constructive toward myself.

I have three main intentions for this semester. First, I will write once a week for at least fifteen minutes, preferably at the same time and in the same quiet place. A regular appointment will make reflection a habit instead of something I do only when I feel overwhelmed. This supports my success in class because I will have a reliable place to process course ideas, and it supports my life outside class by creating a pause between one week and the next. Second, I will connect events with emotions, body signals, and behavior. Instead of writing only “I had a stressful day,” I want to ask what caused the stress, where I felt it, how I responded, and what I might try next time. This can improve my self-awareness and help me communicate more clearly with other people. Third, I will record small experiments and review them later. I might try a different study schedule, a boundary around sleep, or a new way of asking for help. Looking back will show whether the change helped rather than relying on a vague impression. These goals matter because leadership begins with the ability to notice one's own choices and their effects.

## Part Two: Media Response

The Thrive Global article argues that many students have learned to measure their worth through grades, accomplishments, and comparison. The story of a student who left a hospital because she feared missing class made the danger of perfectionism concrete. I recognize the pressure even though my situation is different. In my family and in my previous educational environment, strong grades were often treated as evidence that a student was responsible and that the family's sacrifices were worthwhile. As a transfer student, I can also feel pressure to prove that I belong at U-M. The article's perspective expands my definition of a successful college experience. Grades matter, but success also includes health, meaningful relationships, curiosity, and the ability to recover when a plan fails.

One boundary I want to practice is a protected period before sleep in which I stop schoolwork and avoid comparing myself with other students online. I can use a realistic weekly plan, leave space for rest, and ask for help before a problem becomes a crisis. This is not lowering my standards; it is creating conditions in which I can learn for a long time. I hope ALA 240 will help me become a more attentive and humane leader. A leader who understands personal limits is more likely to notice when teammates are struggling, listen without rushing to judge, and build a group where people can ask questions.

The EDUCAUSE reading adds a useful warning about AI assistance. A strategy I can use in ALA 240 is to complete an initial free-write and identify my own examples before opening an AI tool. If I use technology afterward, I can ask it to point out unclear sentences or questions I have missed, then check every suggestion against my experience and the course materials. Writing the first version myself keeps the reflection connected to my memory and judgment. It also lets me see where my thinking is incomplete. In that way, journaling remains a practice of learning and self-care rather than a performance designed only to produce a better-looking answer.


# Week 2 Journal: Mindfulness and Values

*Draft assumption: the two values on my name card were growth and integrity. I will replace them if my actual values were different.*

## Part One

I chose growth and integrity because they describe the kind of student and person I want to become. Growth means remaining willing to learn, receive feedback, and change direction. Integrity means making my actions match my commitments even when no one is checking. Three behaviors support growth for me: I ask specific questions when I do not understand something, I choose challenges that stretch my skills instead of always choosing the easiest option, and I review mistakes for information rather than treating them as proof that I cannot improve. Three behaviors support integrity: I communicate honestly about what I can finish, I give proper credit to other people's work, and I follow through on small promises such as replying, arriving prepared, or completing a task when I said I would.

There are also slippery behaviors that move me away from these values. Growth becomes unhealthy when I compare my progress with everyone else, avoid a difficult task because I might look inexperienced, or treat feedback as a personal judgment. Integrity slips when I say yes to too many responsibilities and then hide that I am behind, take shortcuts without considering whether I am learning, or agree publicly while privately ignoring an important concern. These behaviors can look productive in the moment, which is why they are difficult to notice. The early warning signs for me are rushing, poor sleep, irritability, defensiveness, and the urge to present a perfect result rather than ask for assistance.

One time I was fully living into both values was during a challenging programming project after transferring schools. I did not understand the assignment as quickly as some classmates, and my first solution failed several tests. My first reaction was embarrassment. Instead of copying a solution or pretending I understood, I wrote down the errors, asked a classmate how they approached debugging, and met with the instructor during office hours. I revised the program in smaller steps and explained honestly which parts I still needed to practice. The final result was not perfect, but I learned more than I would have learned from hiding the problem. That experience showed me that growth requires humility and integrity requires truthful communication.

My family knows these values because they have encouraged me to keep learning while taking responsibility for my choices. A close friend from my previous university also supports them. Support looks like asking how a difficult week actually went, reminding me that a grade is information rather than an identity, and encouraging me to contact a professor instead of disappearing when I am confused. My choice of values comes from family expectations about education, lived experiences of moving between countries, and the transition to a university culture that rewards initiative. These values are not just labels; they are a way to decide what deserves my attention.

My main insight from the values exercise is that wellbeing depends on alignment. When my schedule is full but my actions reflect growth and integrity, I feel tired yet grounded. When I achieve something by hiding mistakes or ignoring my limits, success feels fragile. Mindfulness helps me notice the difference before I react automatically. Emotional intelligence gives me a language for naming frustration, shame, or fear and choosing a response that fits my values.

I can practice this alignment in small moments. Before replying to a stressful message, I can notice the urge to defend myself, take one breath, and ask which response reflects integrity. Before beginning a difficult task, I can name a growth intention such as “learn one part clearly” rather than “prove that I am already good at this.” These choices turn values into behavior that another person could actually observe.

## Part Two: Media Response

The Calm article describes meditation as noticing thoughts and emotions without judging or immediately changing them. That idea is appealing but difficult. My mind often jumps to the next assignment or replays a conversation in English. I may become frustrated that I am distracted and then conclude that I am meditating incorrectly. A nonjudgmental approach changes the goal: success is returning attention gently, not emptying my mind. I can begin with five minutes of breath awareness or the S.T.O.P. practice: stop, take a breath, observe, and proceed.

U-M culture often rewards constant action. Intentionally slowing down can protect my focus, sleep, and emotional stability. A short pause before studying may help me identify whether I am genuinely ready to work or simply anxious. I want to schedule five minutes after lunch or before a long study block, connect it to an existing habit, and treat consistency as more important than duration. Slowing down will not remove every demand, but it can help me meet those demands deliberately instead of allowing urgency to control every decision.


# Week 3 Journal: Mental Health, Well-being, and Identity

## Part One

As a Chinese transfer student and a junior studying computer science, I carry several educational and cultural experiences into my understanding of mental health. I spent my first two years of college in China. In elementary, middle, and high school, the strongest messages I heard were to work hard, tolerate pressure, and keep private problems inside the family. Mental health was rarely discussed directly. Stress was often described as a normal price of achievement, while asking for help could be interpreted as not being resilient enough. My family cared about my wellbeing, but conversations usually focused on sleep, grades, or physical symptoms rather than anxiety, loneliness, or identity.

On a U.S. campus, I hear more open messages that counseling, accommodations, peer support, and mental health education are legitimate resources. That is affirming, but it does not erase hesitation. As a transfer student who is still learning American cultural expectations, I sometimes worry about explaining myself clearly or being seen as incapable. My current understanding challenges the idea that mental health is simply a private problem or the absence of a diagnosis. I define it as the changing relationship among emotions, thoughts, body, relationships, purpose, and the conditions in which a person lives. People can be functioning well and still need support. Help-seeking is connected to mental health because early conversation can prevent a manageable problem from becoming an emergency. Asking for help is an act of self-awareness and responsibility, not a failure of independence.

The message also changed across my school years. As a younger student, I mostly heard that adults would handle serious problems and that I should concentrate on school. In high school, the pressure to plan a successful future became stronger, but there was still little vocabulary for loneliness or anxiety. On campus I now hear that wellbeing is part of learning, although the pace of classes can communicate the opposite. Recognizing this contradiction helps me question the idea that I must wait until I am in crisis before using support.

## Part Two: YouSoup and Story Exchange

The YouSoup activity reminded me that “healthy” is not one universal behavior. I often learned to associate health with discipline, exercise, and eating certain foods. Those practices can help, but they also require time, money, transportation, physical ability, and a safe environment. A student working many hours, living with a disability, managing a chronic condition, or caring for family may need a different approach. Health can also include rest, social connection, culturally familiar food, or access to affordable care. My most useful a-ha moment from the Story Exchange was realizing how quickly I assume that a person who is quiet is uninterested. Listening to classmates' stories made me more aware that silence can reflect language processing, past experiences, or a need for safety.

Going forward, I want to ask curious questions before giving advice and to make room for perspectives that are different from mine. If someone's beliefs about wellness differ from my own, I can describe my experience without presenting it as the standard. The YouSoup framework also helps me see that being international and educated in China can be a privilege in some settings and a vulnerability in others. I have access to family support and technical training, yet language, cultural unfamiliarity, and immigration-related uncertainty can make resources harder to navigate.

## Part Three: Media Response

Rhonda Magee's chapter was difficult and clarifying. Her story about Jake and his family's reaction to their interracial relationship showed that racism is not only an individual attitude; it can operate through assumptions, control of safety, and unequal access to belonging. I felt sadness, anger, and surprise while reading. I noticed tension in my chest and shoulders, especially when I imagined how a person could be reduced to a racial category by people who had never tried to know her. Magee's “Pause” asked me to stop, notice my breath and body, and allow the reaction to be present without judging it. My breathing became slower, and I felt more able to distinguish the story itself from my first impulse to move away from discomfort. The follow-up action is to keep learning how race and identity shape institutions, listen to people whose experiences differ from mine, and ask what assumptions I am making before I respond.

Dr. Stephanie Cook's video added a biological dimension to these ideas. I learned that minority stress and discrimination can affect cortisol regulation and that researchers can study stress responses through saliva and hair samples. Chronic social stress can dysregulate the body, while close social bonds can buffer stress and support resilience. I build bonds through regular calls with family, study groups, and friendships with classmates who understand the uncertainty of transfer life. These relationships relieve stress when they provide trust and honest encouragement. They can become stressful when I feel pressure to appear successful or when I do not communicate my limits. Building healthier bonds means sharing more honestly, listening carefully, and setting boundaries that allow connection to remain supportive.


# Week 4 Journal: Community Well-being

## Part One

At the beginning of the semester, I thought of community mainly as a group that shared a place, activity, or identity. Our class discussion expanded that definition. I now see community as an ongoing relationship in which people share responsibility for norms, resources, and belonging. Easy parts for me to practice are respect, reliability, listening, and sharing useful information. More challenging parts are addressing conflict directly and speaking when a familiar group norm may exclude someone. Community well-being requires more than being friendly; it requires noticing who can participate safely and who is carrying invisible barriers.

Before college, my communities included my family, hometown friends, my secondary school, and classmates in a programming group. During college, I belong to my transfer and international-student network, computer science classes, project teams, a study group, and the broader campus community. These groups contain different levels of connection. My family offers long-term care but is far away. A study group offers practical support, yet it can become focused only on grades. A large lecture provides shared purpose but not necessarily psychological safety. Within the larger campus community, subcommunities based on major, language, residence, nationality, or student organizations can create belonging for insiders while making others unsure how to enter.

I feel most welcomed in a small study group where members explain concepts without treating questions as an inconvenience. I can arrive with imperfect English, and the group focuses on helping everyone understand. By contrast, I have felt less welcome in fast-paced discussions where people rely on cultural references or interrupt before I can finish a thought. The difference is not simply personality. The first group practices patience and makes room; the second assumes that confidence and speed equal competence. Those assumptions affect who speaks, whose ideas are developed, and who leaves feeling that they do not belong.

## Part Two

The Academic Wheel of Privilege was new to me as a formal tool. It challenged my assumption that privilege is a single personal trait. As an international transfer student, I have educational privilege through access to U-M, technology, and a computer science curriculum. At the same time, language, immigration status, unfamiliarity with U.S. systems, and the cost of studying here can create barriers. My position affects access to wellbeing resources because I may have insurance and campus services but still need extra time to understand how to use them or to feel comfortable asking. On campus, I may be treated as technically capable while my perspective is overlooked in conversations that move quickly.

I can help bridge gaps by sharing information about tutoring, counseling, financial support, and student organizations in accessible language. I can invite quieter people into a discussion without forcing them to speak, explain hidden expectations to new students, and challenge jokes or assumptions that turn a group into an insiders-only space. Carin Taylor's tenets of belonging—psychological safety, empathy, acceptance, connection, and embracing—give me a concrete standard. I have been in communities where decisions were made before everyone arrived, so people did not feel safe disagreeing. The result was polite silence rather than genuine belonging. A healthier community would ask for input earlier and treat disagreement as information.

## Part Three: Media Response

My initial reaction to Giannis Antetokounmpo's response was relief. He refused to call an entire season a failure simply because the team did not win the championship. I define failure as refusing to learn, refusing to repair harm, or abandoning a meaningful goal without reflection. An unwanted outcome is not automatically a failed life. This definition applies to school and personal life. I can fail a test or misjudge a schedule while still making progress. When friends or family struggle, I want to speak about effort, learning, and next steps rather than reducing them to one result.

A recent challenge was adjusting to a heavy computer science workload while learning the cultural and communication expectations of a new university. Outside my sphere of control are other people's opinions, fixed deadlines, and how quickly a professor responds. Inside my sphere are my study schedule, the questions I ask, whether I attend office hours, how I break a project into smaller tasks, and how I protect sleep. One action I can take this week is to create a realistic task plan and attend office hours before I feel desperate. That action restores agency without pretending that every structural difficulty is under my control.

The content that challenged me most was the idea that a community can be welcoming to me while still being inaccessible to someone with a different intersection of identities. My commitment is to ask who might be missing before making decisions in a group and to offer more than one way to participate. Intersectionality will become a practical habit of community care rather than an abstract term.


# Week 5 Journal: Success and Failure

## Part One

My view of failure was shaped by a Chinese educational environment in which exam results were highly visible and success was often connected to ranking, discipline, and the sacrifices made by family. I learned to treat a mistake as evidence that I needed to work harder, but I also learned to hide mistakes because they could feel embarrassing. Transferring to the United States added another layer. I had to learn new classroom expectations, participate in English, and prove to myself that my previous education was still valuable in a different system. These experiences made me persistent, but they also made it easy to believe that one poor grade could threaten my identity as a capable student.

Class content has challenged that belief. Giannis Antetokounmpo's response shows that an outcome is one step in a longer process, not a complete judgment of a person. Growth-oriented reflection asks what I learned, what conditions shaped the result, and what I can try next. When I feel that I have failed, I first notice the emotions instead of immediately arguing with them. On the emotion wheel, success may bring pride, relief, gratitude, confidence, or excitement. Failure may bring disappointment, shame, anxiety, frustration, sadness, and sometimes curiosity. Naming the feeling helps me separate emotion from identity. I then write two columns: what happened and the story I am telling about what happened. Finally, I contact a trusted person or choose one small next action, such as revising code, asking a question, or making a new plan.

My family background influences this process because education represents opportunity and collective effort. I do not want to dismiss the value of hard work, but I am learning that protecting my wellbeing is part of honoring that effort. As an international transfer student, I also need to resist the idea that I must always be grateful, quiet, or excellent in order to deserve a place here. I can be serious about my goals while still being a person who needs rest and support.

I am also learning to distinguish a failed strategy from a failed self. A study plan can fail because it was unrealistic, a conversation can fail because I did not listen, and a project can fail because I tested too late. Each result gives me information about timing, communication, or preparation. This distinction makes it easier to repair a mistake and to speak to friends with compassion instead of repeating the harsh language I once used with myself.

## Part Two: Media Response

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's “The Danger of a Single Story” made me think about the stories attached to my own identity. One story I received from school, family, and media was that a Chinese student in computer science should be disciplined, good at mathematics, and willing to sacrifice personal comfort for achievement. That story empowered me by giving me confidence in persistence and technical learning. It became limiting when it suggested that I should not struggle publicly, ask for help, or have interests outside academics. Another single story is the assumption that transferring from China means I understand neither American culture nor independent decision-making. My life contains more than one narrative: I am a learner, friend, family member, programmer, multilingual communicator, and person still becoming comfortable in a new environment. Holding multiple stories helps me avoid reducing other people to a category as well.

I see these questions clearly in my computer science classes and study groups. Students with different intersecting identities can experience the same room differently. A domestic student who already knows U.S. classroom norms may speak quickly and find office hours familiar. An international student may need more time to process language, worry about sounding uninformed, or hesitate to challenge a group. A student who is also managing financial pressure, disability, caregiving, gender bias, or racial stereotyping may experience participation in still another way. Access to technology, prior programming courses, transportation, and free time also shapes who can contribute. The community may look equal from the outside while distributing confidence and opportunity unevenly.

My responsibility is not to assume that my experience represents the group. I can explain expectations to new classmates, invite multiple voices, share resources, and notice who is interrupted or left with invisible labor. I can also accept correction when I make an assumption. Success now means building skill without losing curiosity or compassion. Failure can be a painful signal, but it can also be a doorway to a fuller story about who I am and how I want to participate in community.
